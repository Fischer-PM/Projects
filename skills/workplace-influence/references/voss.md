# Voss — Tactical Empathy in the Live Conversation

Chris Voss's negotiation method (from FBI hostage negotiation) is the toolkit for
the **conversation as it happens**: pushback, conflict, distrust, someone who's
already annoyed. Use it when the problem is **relational** — history, friction,
or emotion is in the room — or when the user needs to be prepped to say actual
words under pressure.

Core premise: the emotion has to be addressed before the substance can be. People
who feel unheard argue; people who feel understood negotiate.

## The tools

**Labeling** — name the other person's emotion or position out loud, tentatively.
Openers: *"It seems like…" "It sounds like…" "It looks like you're…"*
Never "I hear that you're" — it makes it about you.

> "It sounds like you've been burned by a migration promise before."
> "It seems like the timing is the real problem here, more than the approach."

Labeling a negative defuses it. Say it, then **stay quiet** and let them respond.
The silence is doing the work; filling it wastes the move.

**Accusation audit** — say their worst objection before they do. This drains it
of force and buys enormous credibility.

> "You're going to think this is Product asking Engineering to absorb risk again,
> and that the last time we did this it slipped two quarters. Both fair."

**Mirroring** — repeat the last one to three words of what they said, as a
question. Prompts elaboration with zero content from you. Useful when you don't
yet understand the real objection.

> Them: "…it's just not the right time for this."
> You: "Not the right time?"

**Calibrated questions** — open questions starting with *how* or *what* that make
the other person solve your problem. Avoid "why," which reads as accusatory.

> "How am I supposed to get this into the roadmap without your team's capacity?"
> "What would have to be true for this to be worth doing?"
> "What's the biggest challenge you face here?"
> "How would you like me to proceed?"

The "how am I supposed to…" form is the strongest polite no available. It
declines without refusing, and hands them the constraint.

**"That's right" over "you're right"** — the goal is getting them to say *that's
right* after you summarize their position back to them. It signals genuine
agreement. *"You're right"* usually means they want you to stop talking.

**No-oriented questions** — people feel safe saying no. Design questions where no
is the productive answer.

> "Is it a ridiculous idea to pilot this with one team first?"
> "Have you given up on hitting the Q4 date?"

**Tone** — calm, slow, downward-inflected. Voss's "late-night FM DJ voice."
Deliberate calm is contagious and signals you aren't threatened. The upward
inflection on a statement signals uncertainty; drop it.

**Silence** — after a label, after an offer, after a hard question. Four to six
seconds feels endless and is usually where the other person reveals the real
objection. This is the tool users most consistently skip.

## Sequencing a hard conversation

1. **Accusation audit** — name the objections and history up front.
2. **Label** what you believe they're feeling, then stop.
3. **Mirror and ask calibrated questions** until you understand the actual
   constraint (which is usually not the stated one).
4. **Summarize** their position until you get *"that's right."*
5. **Only then** introduce your ask, framed inside their constraint.
6. **Calibrated question** to hand them the implementation problem.

## Where users go wrong

- Leading with their case instead of the other person's position.
- Treating the first stated objection as the real one. It rarely is — the stated
  objection is often technical when the real one is capacity, headcount, credit,
  or a prior burn.
- Rushing to fill silence.
- Getting emotional escalation confused with substantive disagreement. Address
  the emotion first, always.

## When Voss is the wrong lens

If there's no friction and no history, this is overkill and can read as
technique-y with people who know the material. Straightforward asks deserve
straightforward language.
