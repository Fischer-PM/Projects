# Greene — Reading People and Power Realism

Robert Greene's work is the **diagnostic** lens: what is this person actually
motivated by, beneath what they say? Use it when the problem is **motivational**
— the user is confused by someone's behavior, or their read of a stakeholder is
naive.

## How to use this material responsibly

Two caveats to hold while coaching from it:

**It's observation, not science.** *The Laws of Human Nature* and *The 48 Laws of
Power* are syntheses of history, biography, and psychology, not peer-reviewed
research. Present them as a lens for generating hypotheses about someone's
motives, then test those hypotheses against evidence. Cialdini's principles carry
empirical weight; Greene's carry interpretive usefulness. Don't blur the two.

**Much of the power material is explicitly amoral.** Greene wrote *48 Laws*
descriptively, and several laws are straightforwardly ruthless. In a workplace
context, use it in two modes only:

- **Diagnostic** — understanding what's driving someone's behavior.
- **Defensive** — recognizing when a dynamic is being used on the user, and
  protecting their position.

Do not coach the offensive versions — undermining a colleague, cultivating
dependence, manufacturing a rival's failure. If a user asks for that, redirect:
"Here's what I think that person is doing and why, and here's how you protect
your position" is nearly always what they actually need, and it's the version
that doesn't blow up on them.

## Reading people accurately

The central skill: people present a curated version of themselves. Judge from
**patterns of behavior over time**, especially under pressure, not from stated
intentions. What someone did the last three times they were squeezed predicts the
fourth better than anything they say in a meeting.

Practical reads:

- **Actions over words, always.** The stakeholder who says they support the
  initiative but never sends the email does not support the initiative.
- **Watch the moment of pressure.** Character shows when something is at stake —
  who gets thrown under the bus during an incident, who takes credit in the recap.
- **Attend to the small tells.** What they complain about repeatedly, what they
  bring up unprompted, whose name they mention. These reveal the actual priority
  more reliably than their stated OKRs.
- **Assume self-interest is present without assuming it's the whole story.**
  Most people are neither cynical operators nor pure altruists; they are
  protecting something. Figure out what.

## Motives that commonly drive workplace resistance

When someone's objection doesn't make sense on the stated terms, one of these is
usually underneath. Generate the hypothesis, then look for evidence:

| Underlying motive | How it presents |
|---|---|
| **Threat to status or scope** | Sudden concern about process, governance, or "alignment" |
| **Envy** | Excessive criticism of your work's details; praise that undercuts; enthusiasm that never converts to support |
| **Fear of being exposed** | Resistance to measurement, visibility, or anything that reveals current state |
| **Loss of control** | Objections to sequencing and ownership rather than substance |
| **Prior burn** | Vague, immovable resistance with no clear technical basis — someone promised this before and didn't deliver |
| **No incentive** | Politeness, agreement in the room, no action after |
| **Grandiosity** | Needs the idea to be theirs; resists anything that arrives fully formed from elsewhere |

The last one has a well-known practical implication (Greene's "never outshine the
master"): if a senior person needs ownership, giving them the idea to improve is
often the difference between a yes and a slow no. This is legitimate — the
outcome is what the user wants, the credit isn't the point. It becomes
manipulation only if the user is lying about the substance.

## Power realism, used defensively

The parts worth internalizing for a workplace, without the ruthlessness:

- **You are in the game whether or not you play.** Declining to think about
  organizational dynamics doesn't exempt the user from them; it just makes them
  worse at navigating.
- **Say less than necessary.** Especially in unfamiliar rooms and especially
  before you understand the dynamics. Overexplaining reveals uncertainty and
  gives others material.
- **Observe the implicit rules before acting.** New team, new org, new senior
  leader — the written rules are not the operating ones. Watch first.
- **Plan to the end.** Before making a move, think through what happens after it
  succeeds. Users frequently win the argument and lose the relationship, or get
  the yes and inherit an unresourced mandate.
- **Recognize being used.** If someone is consistently extracting the user's work
  without reciprocity or credit, name it internally and change the terms. The
  defensive read matters more than the offensive one.

## The tone to strike

Greene material invites cynicism, which is a bad state for the user to operate
from — it corrodes relationships and reads badly. The useful register is
**clear-eyed, not cynical**: assume people are protecting something legitimate,
be honest about interests, and still choose to act with integrity. That's the
combination that actually compounds over a career.

Primary sources: *The Laws of Human Nature*; *The 48 Laws of Power*; *Mastery*.
