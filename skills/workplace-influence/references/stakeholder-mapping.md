# Mapping the Field

Use this when the situation involves more than one person, or when the user
doesn't yet know where to aim. Most failed influence attempts are aimed at the
wrong person.

## Find the actual decider

Ask directly: **whose signature or nod makes this real?** Then separate the roles,
because they're frequently different people:

- **Decider** — can say yes and make it stick.
- **Blocker** — can't say yes, can say no. (Often finance, security, legal,
  architecture, or a peer whose roadmap is affected.)
- **Influencer** — the person the decider calls before deciding. Frequently
  invisible on the org chart and the highest-leverage target in the whole map.
- **Implementer** — has to do the work; can kill it slowly through deprioritization.
- **Beneficiary** — gets value; your best source of social proof and advocacy.

The most common user error: spending all effort on the decider while ignoring the
influencer they'll consult and the implementer who will quietly deprioritize it.

## Power / interest grid

Place each stakeholder:

|  | **Low interest** | **High interest** |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | Keep satisfied — brief, no surprises; they'll notice if blindsided | **Manage closely** — pre-wire, co-create, give ownership |
| **Low power** | Monitor — low effort | Keep informed — your advocates and social proof |

The high-power/low-interest quadrant is where people get ambushed. Someone with
authority who doesn't care about your project will still object loudly if they
first learn about it in a room full of their peers. A two-minute heads-up
prevents this entirely.

## Per-stakeholder read

For each person who matters, answer four things:

1. **Measured on** — what shows up in their performance review?
2. **Worried about** — what's the thing that keeps surfacing in their comments?
3. **Costs them** — what does saying yes actually take from them (headcount,
   attention, risk, control, credit)?
4. **History** — what happened last time someone asked them for something like this?

If the user can't answer #3, that's usually the missing piece. Every yes has a
cost to the person giving it, and unnamed costs become unexplained resistance.

## Sequencing

Order the conversations deliberately — this is pre-suasion applied to a group:

1. **Allies first.** Confirm support, refine the pitch, collect specific
   objections you'll hear later.
2. **The influencer next.** Get them bought in before the decider consults them.
3. **The likely blocker before the meeting, privately.** Surface their objection
   where they can raise it without losing face, and let them shape the mitigation.
   A blocker who helped design the mitigation defends it instead of attacking it.
4. **The decider**, ideally already hearing about it from two other directions.
5. **The room** — where you ratify a decision that has effectively already been
   made, not where you make it.

If the user is about to make a real decision for the first time in a large
meeting, that's usually the single highest-value thing to change about their plan.

## Coalition math

Ask: who else has this problem? Influence attempts framed as one team's need
compete with every other team's need. The same request framed as a shared problem
across three teams changes category — it stops being a roadmap item and starts
being an org issue. Finding one or two peer teams with the same pain is often
worth more than any amount of message refinement.
