# Cialdini — Principles of Influence

Robert Cialdini's principles are the best-evidenced material in this skill —
decades of field and lab research. Use this lens when the problem is **framing
or motivation**: the idea is sound, the user has adequate standing, but the ask
isn't landing.

Two rules for applying them well:

1. **Pick one or two.** A plan built on the two principles that genuinely fit
   beats a plan that name-checks all seven. Heavy-handed stacking creates
   resistance — people can feel it.
2. **The principle must be true.** Fabricated scarcity, invented social proof,
   or borrowed authority works once and costs credibility permanently. In an
   enterprise where the user has to work with these people for years, that
   trade is always bad.

## The seven principles, in workplace form

**Reciprocity** — people repay what they receive. Give first: unblock someone
else's project, share a useful analysis, cover for them, make an introduction.
The strongest form is unexpected, personalized, and given before you need
anything. *Workplace read:* the most influential PMs are usually the ones with
outstanding favors across the org.

**Commitment & Consistency** — people act consistently with what they've
previously said, especially publicly and in writing. Get small agreements
first: "does the problem framing seem right to you?" before "will you fund
this?" Reflecting someone's stated priority back to them and building the
proposal on it makes rejection feel inconsistent with their own position.
*Workplace read:* this is why pre-wiring works and why a documented "yes, this
is the priority" from a stakeholder is worth more than a good deck.

**Social Proof** — people look to similar others under uncertainty. Which
peer teams have done this? What's the industry norm? Adoption numbers, other
executives already on board, a comparable company's approach. Most powerful
when the proof comes from people the target sees as *similar* to themselves —
a peer director's endorsement beats an abstract industry stat.

**Authority** — people defer to credible expertise, and credibility must be
established before the ask, not asserted during it. Have someone else introduce
your expertise; cite the constraint you've personally worked through; show the
scars. *Counterintuitive move:* mentioning a genuine weakness in your proposal
early increases trust in everything else you say. Candor about the downside is
one of the cheapest credibility purchases available.

**Liking** — people say yes to those they like: genuine similarity, real
compliments, and cooperation toward a shared goal. In practice this means the
five minutes before the meeting matter, and it means finding the thing you
actually have in common rather than manufacturing rapport.

**Scarcity** — people value what's limited and are more moved by potential loss
than equivalent gain. **Loss framing is the single most reliable reframe in
enterprise settings:** what does the org lose by not doing this, what's the cost
of the status quo, what window closes. "This unlocks $2M" lands softer than
"we're bleeding $2M a year and this stops it." Only use it when the loss is real.

**Unity** — shared identity ("one of us"), not just similarity. Same team, same
mission, same fight. This is the accelerator: when unity is present, every other
principle works harder. *Workplace read:* "we" framing that's genuinely earned —
shared ownership of the problem and the outcome — changes the conversation from
your-ask-my-cost to our-problem.

## Pre-suasion: the highest-leverage part

Cialdini's later work argues that what happens *before* the ask often determines
the outcome more than the ask itself. Whatever a person's attention is on at the
moment of decision is perceived as more important and more causal.

Practical applications:

- **Pre-wire.** Never let a real decision happen first in a big meeting. Talk to
  each key person beforehand, surface objections privately, and let them shape
  it. People support what they helped build.
- **Set the frame.** Open by establishing the criteria for a good decision
  before presenting your option. If the room agrees speed matters most, the
  fast option wins on their terms, not yours.
- **Sequence deliberately.** Present the problem and its cost before the
  solution and its price. Ask the small question before the big one.
- **Choose the moment.** Timing relative to budget cycles, incidents,
  performance reviews, and org changes is a real variable, not an afterthought.

## Choosing which principle fits

| Situation | Usually strongest |
|---|---|
| Stakeholder doesn't know or trust you | Authority, Liking, Unity |
| They agree in principle, won't commit | Commitment & Consistency, Scarcity |
| They're risk-averse / status-quo biased | Scarcity (loss framing), Social Proof |
| Cross-team turf resistance | Unity, Reciprocity |
| Exec with no time and many options | Scarcity, Social Proof, Authority |
| You need a favor with no leverage | Reciprocity (give first, wait) |

## Ethical line

Cialdini's own framing: these principles describe how people naturally decide.
Using them to direct attention toward true and relevant features of a good
proposal is legitimate. Using them to make a bad proposal feel good is
manipulation, and it also selects for outcomes the user will have to live with.

Primary sources: *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*; *Pre-Suasion*.
