# Pfeffer — Power and Positioning

Jeffrey Pfeffer (Stanford GSB) writes about how power is actually acquired in
organizations, as opposed to how people wish it were. Use this lens when the
user's problem is **structural**: they lack standing, access, visibility, or
control over anything anyone needs. No amount of message-crafting fixes that.

## The core, uncomfortable premise

Performance and advancement are only loosely coupled. Good work is necessary and
insufficient. What converts work into influence is whether the people who
allocate resources know about it, attribute it to you, and see you as someone
whose judgment should be sought.

The corollary that matters most for coaching: **if the user is doing excellent
work and being ignored, the fix is usually visibility and positioning, not more
excellent work.** Users in this situation often resist that advice as
distasteful. Acknowledge the discomfort, then be honest that the alternative is
continuing to be overlooked.

Related: the world is not just. Waiting to be noticed on merit is a strategy
that depends on other people's diligence about noticing.

## Where power comes from

Diagnose which of these the user has, and which they can build:

| Source | What it looks like | How to build it |
|---|---|---|
| **Resource control** | Owning budget, headcount, a system others depend on | Take on the unglamorous thing everyone needs and nobody owns |
| **Network centrality** | Being the connection point between groups that don't talk | Deliberately span structural holes — be the bridge, not a node in one cluster |
| **Information** | Knowing what's happening before others do | Be in the pre-meetings; maintain weak ties across the org |
| **Formal authority** | Title, reporting line, decision rights | Slow to change; rarely the user's near-term lever |
| **Allies and sponsors** | Senior people invested in your success | Ask for advice (not favors); make them look good; be useful to them repeatedly |
| **Reputation** | What people say when you're not there | Consistency, visible reliability, a known area of expertise |
| **Personal qualities** | Energy, focus, tolerance for conflict, self-knowledge | Practice; especially the conflict tolerance, which is the rarest |

The most actionable for an individual contributor without authority are usually
**network centrality**, **information**, and **sponsors** — all buildable
without permission.

## Sponsors vs. mentors

A mentor gives advice. A sponsor spends their own capital advocating for you in
rooms you're not in. Users routinely have mentors and no sponsors, then wonder
why nothing changes. Sponsors are earned by making yourself useful to someone
powerful — solving a problem they own, making them look good, being reliably
competent on something they care about.

Practical move: asking a senior person for **advice** is a low-cost request that
creates engagement and mild investment in your success (this is also Cialdini's
commitment-and-consistency at work). Asking for a favor cold is a high-cost
request from someone with no stake in you.

## Visibility without being insufferable

The failure mode users fear is being seen as a self-promoter. Ways to be visible
that don't read as bragging:

- Report outcomes and their business consequence, not activity.
- Credit the team specifically while being clearly the person who drove it.
- Be the one who summarizes, synthesizes, and sends the recap — the person who
  writes the summary shapes what people remember and becomes associated with it.
- Volunteer for the cross-org problem nobody wants; it's the fastest route to
  both resource control and network centrality.
- Speak early in meetings. People who speak first are read as more central.

## Diagnostic questions

- Who decides on this, and what are they measured on?
- Does anyone senior have a stake in the user's success? Who?
- What does the user control that others need? (If "nothing," that's the finding.)
- Who does the user know outside their immediate team?
- Are they visible to the people who allocate opportunity, or only to their peers?

## When Pfeffer is the wrong lens

If the user has real standing and a healthy relationship with the other party,
don't turn a solvable conversation into a power-building project. Pfeffer is for
diagnosing why influence attempts keep failing at a structural level, and for
long-game positioning — not for every meeting.

Primary source: *Power: Why Some People Have It and Others Don't*; *7 Rules of
Power*.
